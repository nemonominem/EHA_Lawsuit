    About this page

    About this page. It is two things in one: a guide to the court record in Daszak v. EcoHealth Alliance, and an analysis of the case and of some of the claims the plaintiff makes.

    • For a quick overview: read the "broke" tab, then "The dispute", which tells the story in four short paragraphs and a timeline.
    • For research: the documents sit in the document pane on the right, under two tabs: Court Fillings (all 71 filings) and 990s (EHA's Form 990s, Nature Health Global's return and the Wallace Research Foundation's returns). Every number in brackets in the text, such as (65), opens the filing it comes from, and a year such as 2024-25 opens its tax return. "The legal pipeline" maps the documents by stage, from the complaint to the appeal.
    • What is record and what is reading: the documents and the public filings are the record. The conclusions, headed "My analysis" and "Conclusion", are the author's reading of them.

    Case: Dr. Peter Daszak, Ph.D. v. EcoHealth Alliance Inc., Stephen Shapiro and 17 other directors New York Supreme Court, New York County, Commercial Division (Part 54, Justice Jennifer G. Schecter), Index No. 164611/2025.

    Record covered: NYSCEF docs 1–71, filed Nov 2025 – Aug 2026. Numbers in the text are NYSCEF document numbers.

    Where it stands (25 Aug 2026):

    • Daszak's central claim is that he was unfairly dismissed, and that this causes him terrible harm because he is "broke".
    • The trial court dismissed the entire case at the pleading stage on 28 Jul 2026.
    • Daszak filed a notice of appeal to the Appellate Division, First Department.

    The "broke" claim: what the record shows

    My analysis

    After earning about $6.2 million at EHA over sixteen years, and with the house having gone up in value by roughly $900,000 since 2015, it is hard to believe he is broke, unless he managed his finances as badly as he managed his NGO.

    Let's go through the claim and the numbers.

    Where the claim is made

    • Sworn pleading. The verified complaint says Daszak "remains unemployed and is now poor" (54) and "was unable to secure new employment" (65). Daszak swore to the complaint.
    • On the tape (65), in his words:
      • Money and savings:
        • "We have no savings. We have no options."
        • "no job, no backup plan, no savings, no pension, and a mortgage"
        • "You know, you paid me well, but we don't have savings. We don't have commercial. We don't have stock accounts, and um, and, um, you know, and side gigs and all this stuff. ... This is all I've ever had."
        • "I don't have enough money in a retirement to retire."
      • Mortgage, family and the house:
        • "I've got a mortgage, The mortgage payment's got to be made. ... The college fees have got to be paid, or what do we do?"
        • "we have to put the house on the market"
      • Severance and survival:
        • "Give me the benefit of that severance package because without it, I am lost."
        • "It's an ability to survive."
        • "that severance gives me a year to be able to get a job. ... I mean, we're doomed otherwise."
        • "I really thought the severance would be there with it. Because without that, I am utterly doomed."
        • "And now I'm out on the street."
      • On pension, in the same exchange, Shapiro says "EcoHealth has a pension," and Daszak answers that he would get "a fraction of the salary" at 67.
    • Legal weight. None of the three claims depends on it. It is context for damages and sympathy, and the judge never mentioned it.

    The money: what the two houses say about "no savings"

    Daszak told the board, on the call of 6 January 2025 (65):

    "You know, you paid me well, but we don't have savings. We don't have commercial. We don't have stock accounts, and um, and, um, you know, and side gigs and all this stuff. Is just, this is it. This is all I've ever had."

    The two sales and purchases show how that sits with the record. His mortgage terms are not in the record, so the figures below are an illustration with typical terms.

    • What he was paid. EHA's returns show $1.77 million in the six years to June 2015 (2009-10 to 2014-15), and $4.38 million in the ten years from July 2015 to June 2025, both before tax. His earlier years at EHA are not in the returns reviewed.
    • First house, bought with 10% down. He puts $29,350 down on the $293,500 price and borrows $264,150 over 30 years at 7%, a usual rate in late 2001. The payment is about $1,760 a month. By August 2015 the balance is about $205,000.
    • First house, sold at a gain, tax included. The sale at $445,000 costs about 6% in agent's fees and closing costs, including New York's transfer tax, about $26,700. The taxable gain is then about $124,800 ($445,000 less costs less the $293,500 price). Federal law excludes up to $250,000 of gain on a main home ($500,000 for a married couple filing jointly), so no tax is due on it. After the $205,000 mortgage, he walks away with about $213,000 in cash.
    • Villa, bought with a mortgage. Put the $213,000 into the $665,000 villa and he borrows about $452,000, 68% of the price, at 4% over 30 years. The payment is about $2,160 a month, or $25,900 a year, plus roughly $30,000 a year in property tax. That is a small part of an annual pay of $350,000 to $500,000.
    • Where it stands in January 2025. The balance would be about $363,000, against an estimated value of $1.5 million to $1.65 million. Clearing it would take about 8% of his gross pay over the period.
    • If he sold the villa. At $1.6 million, less 6% in costs ($96,000), his gain is about $839,000. After the $500,000 exclusion, roughly $340,000 is taxable, which would cost about $87,000 in federal and New York tax at typical rates. After the mortgage, he would still keep about $1.05 million.

    What this adds up to.
    Daszak has done very well out of his own real estate. He most likely holds a house with roughly $1.2 million of equity, and not just any house: a grand colonial revival home from 1908, with luxury features and beautiful grounds, not that far from the ski resorts he likes so much. And all the while he was paid a cosy $6.15 million over his last sixteen years at EHA, an NGO that lived on donations and public grants.

    Conclusion: a pattern the board could not rely on

    Daszak told the board, and then the court, that he was broke. The record shows sixteen years of reported pay of $6.15 million, a house bought for $665,000 that is now worth well over a million, and a new organisation of which he is president, funded by private donors. Taken alone, a loose word about money would mean little. The same gap between what he says and what the record shows runs through the whole case.

    • "No savings," "no pension." On the tape he says he has neither. EHA ran a staff retirement plan: its 990s report $300,240 of retirement and deferred compensation for him over sixteen years, and the minimum the record supports is higher still — at least about $428,000 of contributions, roughly $349,000 of it from EHA (the Annexe sets out how). The chair confirms it on the call: "EcoHealth has a pension."
    • "Unemployed and poor." He swore to this in the complaint, the same document that names him as president of Nature Health Global and editor-in-chief of the journal EcoHealth. NHG's own return shows $37,500 of pay for a ten-hour week.
    • "The board never identified the cause." The complaint says so. On the call the chair gives the reason, "loss of confidence," and EHA gave HHS the same reason a week later.
    • "Without the severance I am doomed." The severance clause covers staff laid off for non-performance reasons, against a signed release. He was asking for it when HHS had found the organisation's own failures behind the loss of funds: late progress reports and unreported risky experiments in Wuhan.
    • A board that never answered. In 2021 ten scientists and academics, among them Jamie Metzl and Gilles Demaneuf, wrote to the board asking for his removal. The board did not reply publicly.
    • No money to collect from EHA — but that is not who he is suing. EHA's final 990 shows net assets of $1.14M falling to zero, "no remaining assets to distribute," and $152,049 of legal fees paid to Nutter McClennen & Fish in December 2025. A win against EHA alone would be a win against an empty shell. But the suit does not rest on EHA: Daszak named the eighteen directors personally and demanded their insurance policies (38), so what he is really pursuing is the directors and EHA's directors-and-officers cover, not the dissolved nonprofit's assets.

    Each point can be checked against the documents cited on this page. The board acted on its own grounds on 2 January 2025, before the lawsuit. What he has said and sworn since fits its reading and bears out its decision. A chief executive who presents the facts as suit the moment, to funders, to his board and then to a judge, is not someone a board can trust to speak for the organisation, least of all one facing debarment.

    Annexe — the detailed numbers

    This annexe holds the technical detail behind the two tables above: why the retirement minimum is not double counting, how Daszak's own share is read, and the wider breakdown of the pay.

    Why the retirement minimum is not double counting

    • The 5% is not inside base plus bonus. The handbook says EHA contributes 5% "of an employee's annual salary" and that this is "independent of any employee withholding." It is paid on top of salary. Base and bonus (columns B(i) and B(ii)) are what he was paid, so taking 5% of them does not take 5% of an amount that already contains the 5%.

    • Each dollar sits in one column. In those three years the 5% is in column (D), not in column (C). The retirement table counts it once, in the starred figure, in place of the $0. It is not added to anything else in that row.

    • Pay is unchanged. The $6.15M total is the sum of every column, so it already contains the 5%, wherever EHA put it. Moving it from "Other benefits" to "Retirement" would change the split, not the total. The $48,569 is added only to the retirement tally, never to pay. The retirement figures and the pay figures are not to be added together.

    • Reference figure. Employer plus employee contributions of at least $428,164 over sixteen years. Of that, $300,240 is what EHA reported in column C, $48,569 is the 5% floor in the three years reported as $0, and $79,355 is Daszak's own minimum. Without the doubtful 2024-25 year it is $381,300.

    • How his own share is worked out. For each year with a reported figure it is the reported amount minus 5% of base plus bonus, capped at 3% of base plus bonus (the most the match can be). From 2019-20 the reported amount is about 8% of base plus bonus, so the full match was being paid. That needs him to have put in at least 3% himself, about $11K to $13K a year. In the three $0 years the match cannot be read, so his share is left at $0.

    • Assumptions. The handbook rule applied in every year (the copy in the record is from 2024), the plan's "salary" includes the bonus, and the match is dollar for dollar. His own contributions are not reported on the 990, so that part is inferred, and it is a floor.

    • 2024-25. The $35,487 equals exactly 8% of the 2023-24 base plus bonus, so it was probably carried over and the 2024-25 figure may be too high.

    The wider picture

    1. Scale.
      • Sixteen years of reported compensation total $6.15M, an average of $384K a year.
      • The last ten years total $4.38M.
      • The last five (2020-21 to 2024-25) total $2.40M, about $479K a year.
      • The peak was $498K in 2022-23.
    2. Retirement.
      • The returns report $32K to $35K a year of retirement and deferred compensation from 2020-21: $169,838 over the five years. The 2024-25 figure is identical to 2023-24 ($35,487), which may be a carried-over number.
      • The returns answer "No" to a supplemental nonqualified retirement plan every year, so there was no executive-only pension.
      • EHA ran a staff retirement plan. The employee handbook (52, section 14.6) describes a TIAA-CREF plan to which EHA contributes 5% of salary, plus a match of the first 3% of the employee's own contribution, and EHA's cash-flow sheet carries a "Pension Plan - TIAA CREF" line.
      • On the call Daszak says he has "no pension" and that he does not "have enough money in a retirement to retire." Shapiro answers "EcoHealth has a pension," and Daszak then speaks of what he would get at 67: "a fraction of the salary I'm getting now."
      • The returns do not show the plan's balance. They report $300,240 of retirement and deferred compensation over the sixteen years. The table above puts the minimum the record supports, with the 5% floor in the $0 years and Daszak's own contributions added, at $428,164.
      • Whether any of it is a defined-benefit pension is not in the record.
    3. A puzzle in the last return.
      • The 2024-25 return reports $379K of base pay for a year in which Daszak was fired on 6 Jan, six months in. That is close to a full year's salary, and the same return says "No" to any severance payment.
      • Possible explanations: pay continued to the end of the fiscal year, accrued leave was paid out, or the figure was reported on a different basis. The return does not say which.
      • If salary did continue, it would sit uneasily with the picture of being "thrown out on the street."
      • The complaint also says Daszak stayed on the board until June 2025.
    4. What the EHA 990s cannot show. Spouse's income, savings, other assets, debts, legal bills, or whether the 2020-21 to 2023-24 pay was spent. The sworn complaint itself says Daszak is "President of the nonprofit Nature Health Global" and Editor-in-Chief of the Springer-Nature journal EcoHealth (7), next to "unemployed." Part 4 covers that return.

    The dispute

    Summary

    The story

    Peter Daszak ran EcoHealth Alliance (EHA) for 24 years, according to the complaint. His CV (3) dates his presidency of EHA from 2009 to 2025. Before COVID he was not well known outside virology. EHA presented itself as a small New York nonprofit studying bat viruses, but it worked inside the US biodefense research effort and took part in "Track II" scientific exchanges with China. It lived on federal money, and its largest funder was the Pentagon, mostly through the Defense Threat Reduction Agency: about $39 million between 2013 and 2020 (Husseini, Dec 2020). Then COVID arrived, and Congress and federal agencies went after the group and Daszak over research it had funded in Wuhan.

    On 30 September 2021, ten scientists and academics from the Paris Group, an informal network pressing for a full inquiry into the origins of COVID, wrote to EHA's board asking it to remove Daszak as president. Jamie Metzl and Gilles Demaneuf were among the co-authors (the letter, on ResearchGate; Undark's account). They accused Daszak of hiding conflicts of interest and of misleading the public about what he knew of the Wuhan lab's work, and warned that if he stayed he would take EHA down with him. The board never answered publicly. FOIA documents show it asked the signatories to keep quiet for a while, and Metzl told Undark the letter had been "met with silence."

    The board moved only when the government did. After HHS suspended the group in May 2024, funding froze, and over the following months Daszak laid off most of the staff on the board's instruction, with two weeks' severance for each year of service. On 2 January 2025 the board voted to terminate him. On 6 January, in a Zoom call that Daszak recorded, the chair told him he was out for "loss of confidence," offering six months of health cover, relocation costs and consulting at $300 an hour in exchange for a waiver of claims, but none of the severance the laid-off staff had received. HHS debarred EHA and Daszak for five years eleven days later, and EHA was dissolved by June 2026.

    Daszak sued in November 2025 for age discrimination and breach of contract. On 28 July 2026 a judge dismissed the case from the bench at oral argument, with no discovery, and Daszak filed a notice of appeal on 25 August.

    The basic timeline

    • May 2024: the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) suspended EcoHealth Alliance and Daszak and proposed their debarment, over grant and oversight violations tied to research in Wuhan.
    • 6 January 2025: EcoHealth Alliance fired Daszak. The board had voted to terminate him on 2 January.
    • 17 January 2025: HHS formally debarred EcoHealth Alliance and Daszak from federal funding for five years.
    • 3 November 2025: Daszak filed his lawsuit in New York Supreme Court against EcoHealth Alliance and its directors, for age discrimination and breach of contract.
    • 28 July 2026: the court dismissed the case. Daszak filed a notice of appeal on 25 August 2026.

    The cast

    • Peter Daszak: 59 at the firing. His CV (3) lists him as President of EHA from 2009 to 2025 and before that as Executive Director of the Consortium for Conservation Medicine, 2001 to 2009. The complaint says he was EHA's president and CEO for 24 years. EHA's 2008-09 return already names him as principal officer. He was also a board member and the named principal investigator on the NIH grant that sent money to the Wuhan Institute of Virology. The complaint says he has raised over $150 million from donors, corporations, foundations and federal agencies.
    • EcoHealth Alliance: a New York-based nonprofit that lived on federal grants (about 80% of revenue, per the complaint).
    • Stephen Shapiro: board chair, who delivered the news.
    • Ellen Shedlarz: vice chair, on the call.
    • 17 other directors: all sued personally. The defence says they are unpaid volunteers.
    • Lawyers: Joseph Gasperetti for Daszak; Joseph Anci (Jackson Lewis) for the defence.

    The board

    Daszak chose his directors for their connections and their ability to raise money or open doors for EHA. The result was a board of fundraisers and network-builders more than a body that watched over the organisation.     The settlement line, unpacked

    "We're in the process of closing [a settlement]... the boilerplate from the government"

    • What it is not. A money settlement.
    • What it is. An administrative agreement with the HHS suspension and debarment official.
      • Under federal debarment rules (2 C.F.R. § 180.860), an organisation facing debarment can avoid it by promising remedial steps.
      • Typical steps: a compliance programme, an independent monitor, new leadership, and limits on dealing with the person blamed.
    • The paper trail, from the HHS debarment memos:
      • 19 Sep 2024: EHA and Daszak, both represented by Steptoe, jointly propose an agreement.
      • 11 Dec 2024: HHS says the proposal is inconsistent with the record and too thin.
      • 2 Jan 2025: EHA's board votes to terminate Daszak, "because it no longer had confidence in Dr. Daszak's leadership" (EHA's own words to HHS). The Zoom call was four days later.
      • 7 Jan: EHA tells HHS that Steptoe is out, Arnold & Porter is in, and Daszak "was no longer employed."
      • 13 Jan: EHA and Daszak file separate responses.
        • EHA's new agreement lists Daszak's termination as the first of five remedial steps.
        • It promises Daszak "will not have any involvement in the operation or management of EcoHealth Alliance."
        • Daszak files his own proposal through Steptoe.
      • 15 Jan: meeting with HHS.
      • 17 Jan: HHS debars both EHA and Daszak for five years.
    • HHS's verdict on the firing.
      • The debarring official writes that the termination and a meaningful agreement came only on 6 Jan "and days thereafter."
      • That was after the May and December notices had each offered a chance to present one.
      • The memo says "EHA's changes came too late, so I do not believe in their authenticity," and treats the timing as an aggravating factor.
    • What it points to.
      1. It matches Daszak's account of the call. "Separate the lawyers" is documented. The cut-off of the phone, systems and consulting ("if the lawyers will even let us") is the "no involvement" promise being put into effect.
      2. The "cause" was not secret. The board gave the same reason (loss of confidence) to Daszak on 6 Jan and to HHS on 13 Jan. That undercuts the complaint's line that no cause was ever stated, but it also shows the reason was framed for a regulator.
      3. The government-conspiracy theory. Gasperetti told the court Daszak was fired "in cahoots with the government agencies." In the HHS memos, HHS did not demand the firing. EHA offered it as its own remedial step.

    What was offered, and what Daszak claimed

    What COBRA is

    • COBRA is the 1985 federal law (Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act). It lets a departing employee and family keep the employer's group health plan, usually for up to 18 months.
    • The former employee normally pays the full premium plus up to 2%. EHA's handbook says the same (§25.6): "The insured will be required to pay the full cost of monthly insurance premiums."

    What EHA offered

    • Health cover: Shedlarz's offer was that EHA would pay six months of COBRA for the whole family. That is a benefit, not cash, and the record gives no dollar value. The 990s report about $42K–$44K a year of non-taxable benefits for Daszak (health and other benefits combined), so six months of family cover was probably well under $22K. This is an estimate, not a figure in the record.
    • Relocation: up to $70,000, reimbursed against receipts, not paid as cash.
    • Consulting: $300 an hour, at EHA's discretion, for any help EHA called on.
    • A new phone.
    • A "separation agreement ... in formation," which is not in the record. No lump-sum figure is stated anywhere, and the transcript on the "pay heavily, so you don't get taxed" line is garbled.

    All of it was conditioned on a signed waiver of claims (65).

    Where Daszak's $380K comes from

    • On the call: "So a lack of confidence then cost me $380,000."
    • In the affidavit (52): "$368K severance (my prior base, without the bonuses I regularly received)."
    • The figure is a formula, and it lands close to last year's base pay.
      • The 990s show base pay of $383,590 in 2023-24 (the figure on the CFO's August 2024 report) and $379,239 in 2024-25.
      • The handbook's formula is "up to two weeks of severance pay for each year of service."
      • On the call's 25 years, 2 weeks × 25 = 50 weeks. $383,590 × 50/52 = $368,837, about nine months of his total pay.
      • So $368K is 50 weeks of base pay, and $380K on the call is the same number rounded up. It sits close to the $379K reported for the last year because 50 weeks is 96% of a year. It is not derived from the $379K.
    • Years of service: the complaint says 24 years, the call says 25, and the CV (3) gives 2009 to 2025, sixteen years. The formula gives about $368K at 25 years, about $354K at 24 years, and about $236K at sixteen years. The CV does not show earlier service at EHA, so the record leaves the start date open.
    • The bonus is excluded. The annual "bonus" (about $60K in recent years) is not in the base. The next box covers what the 990s say it is.
    • The source is not in the record.
      • The affidavit says the $368K appears on a "Severance" tab of the 22 Aug 2024 CFO report.
      • The defence reply says the exhibit filed does not contain it, and the number "368" appears nowhere in the filed exhibits.
      • The only severance figure in the exhibits is the $1,060,000 total in the December 2024 cash-flow file, labelled "Unlikely, if forced to close."
    • The handbook language cuts the other way. It says "up to" (so it is discretionary, with no stated cap), covers lay-offs for "non-performance-related reasons," and requires a signed release. The judge relied on this.

    How the court decided

    The judge granted the motion from the bench and issued a one-page order: motion granted, complaint dismissed, stay of discovery denied as moot, clerk to enter judgment. The judge ordered the reasons put on the record, with the transcript filed within 45 days (69). Taking every fact in Daszak's favour, as the standard requires:

    1. The contract claim fails.
      • The handbook says in conspicuous terms that it "is not a contract" and creates no obligations. Employees also sign an acknowledgment that employment is at will. The judge cited Lobosco (NY Court of Appeals).
      • Even if it were a contract, the severance clause (§25.7) covers people laid off for non-performance reasons and requires a signed release.
      • The judge asked how being debarred could not be a performance reason.
    2. The age claims fail.
      • The only age fact pleaded is that Daszak was 59. There was no remark, no comparator in a similar position, and the other fired staff were not debarred.
      • The judge called the rest a "government conspiracy" and "fall person" theory, which is not age discrimination. He said discovery without allegations would be a "fishing expedition."
    3. The bylaw violation (removal from the board without notice) the judge saw as unrelated to severance.
    4. Director immunity. The directors' separate argument (unpaid nonprofit directors are immune absent gross negligence) was not reached. The ruling rested on points 1 and 2.

    Where it stands: a final judgment for the defendants. The only live item is the appeal.

    Why the donor schedule says "RESTRICTED"

    • It is the IRS's mask, not NHG's choice.
      • The PDF is the IRS's own "efile Public Visual Render" (it says so in the header), copied by ProPublica.
      • For a 501(c)(3), the public can see contribution amounts but not donor names and addresses. The IRS strips them from the public copy of Schedule B.
      • In the rendered form the donor name, address, amount and even the organisation-type and rule check-boxes come out blank or as "RESTRICTED."
    • EHA's returns look identical. The 2022-23 and 2023-24 EHA returns show the same single "RESTRICTED" row, even though EHA had many donors. So one row means nothing about how many donors NHG has.
    • What can be inferred. NHG's return includes a Schedule B at all, so it probably had at least one gift of $5,000 or more. The line H box is not legible in the render, so this is an inference. Schedule A reports $0 from "disqualified persons," a group that includes officers, so Daszak and Chmura show no gifts there.

    Method

    The IRS publishes every e-filed Form 990 and 990-PF as open data.

    • The search covered all 312,665 Form 990 and 990-PF returns in the ten 2026 batches.
    • It also covered 49,083 returns filed in 2025 for fiscal years ending April 2025 or later (foundations and funds with mid-year fiscal years).
    • It searched for "Nature Health Global" and EIN 33-3618616.
    • The larger donor-advised funds and named foundations were also checked individually.
    • No return other than NHG's own mentions it. ProPublica's full-text search of e-filed returns gives the same result (two hits, both NHG's own return).

    Why the IRS data cannot confirm them yet

    1. Many foundations' 2025 returns are not due until mid-November 2026, and the IRS data runs to about late October.
    2. Some filers, especially bank-trustee trusts such as Samuel Freeman, put the itemised grant list in a PDF attachment that the IRS data does not contain.
    3. Donor-advised funds list grants of $5,000 or more by recipient but never name their own donors. A Schwab Charitable grant would show the fund, not the person.
    4. Grants below $5,000 are not itemised by donor-advised funds.
